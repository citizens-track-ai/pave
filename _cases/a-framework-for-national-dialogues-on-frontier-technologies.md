# Headline findings
headline-findings: "Key findings from consultations in Morocco include:\n\n\u2022\t\
  Maintaining values and integrity: One consistent theme was a strong desire to ensure\
  \ that \u201CMoroccan values\u201D were maintained through any use of AI, particularly\
  \ ensuring that a strong human component accompanies any use of the technology.\
  \ \n\u2022\tResponsibility: We noted that when asked about \u201Csomething going\
  \ wrong\u201D with AI, responses immediately focused on misuse of the technology,\
  \ with little to no mention of AI hallucination or other malfunction. When mentioned,\
  \ tech malfunction examples were often about non-AI based tech. Throughout the consultations,\
  \ developers were often mentioned to be responsible for AI. However, there was also\
  \ a strong sentiment that the responsibility for mitigating local risks of AI fell\
  \ largely on Moroccans. The reasons behind this sentiment differed: some felt that\
  \ developers had already done what they could to mitigate risk, while others believed\
  \ that technology makers were solely interested in profit and disregarded the risks.\
  \ Trust in developers was stronger among people who were avid users of AI, or who\
  \ worked in AI development. Parents and teachers were among the other groups of\
  \ people mentioned as holding responsibility over AI use.\n\u2022\tStrong desire\
  \ for AI training: Another theme was the importance of access to training on AI.\
  \ While the specific objectives or content of the training were not explicitly outlined\
  \ by participants, there was a clear and consistent emphasis on the importance of\
  \ AI education. \n\nKey findings from consultations in Paraguay include: \n\n\u2022\
  \tPeople consulted in the corporate zone of Asuncion\u2014presumably a more affluent\
  \ demographic\u2014were generally more positive about how AI might impact their\
  \ future than people in the market and rural areas\u2014generally poorer and less\
  \ connected.\n\u2022\tIncreasing levels of education among participants also correlated\
  \ with increasing positive sentiments, decreasing negative sentiments, and decreasing\
  \ sentiments of uncertainty regarding the impacts of the technology. Similarly,\
  \ being male was associated with a slightly higher sentiment.\n\u2022\tConcerns\
  \ about a lack of localization of technology were mentioned in several expert interviews.\
  \ Technology is frequently directly translated from the Global North without adequate\
  \ consideration of its applicability within local contexts, perpetuating a broader\
  \ colonial legacy of resource extraction. Paraguay is a bilingual country with 70%\
  \ of the population speaking both Spanish and Guarani. Guarani is mostly a spoken\
  \ language, resulting in a scarcity of digital text which can be used for training\
  \ datasets, an important component of building AI models. Given that Guarani is\
  \ more prominent among the less digitally connected people, the prominence of language\
  \ skills intersects to create the potential for compounding dysconnectivity.\n"

# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: "**The UNDP Accelerator Labs worked with\
  \ Columbia University\u2019s School of International and Public Affairs (SIPA) to\
  \ create a Framework for Conducting National Dialogues on Frontier Technologies.\
  \ The project aimed to provide tried-and-tested guidance for carrying out conversations\
  \ in order to create space for new dialogues and ultimately diversify inputs to\
  \ global conversations on technologies including AI.**\n\nThe framework was piloted\
  \ in Morocco and Paraguay during the month of March 2024. 200 persons were interviewed\
  \ across 50 individual consultations and four different modalities: in-person workshops,\
  \ virtual workshops, online surveys, and street interviews. \n\nThe Framework proposes\
  \ a way to anchor conversations around participants' recent technological interactions\
  \ to avoid getting lost in abstraction. By avoiding questions like \u201Chow can\
  \ AI be deployed in an ethical manner?\u201D or \u201Chow is AI going to change\
  \ the nature of work?\u201D that beg specification of what is meant by AI and who\
  \ is likely to be impacted, and instead structuring conversations around grounded\
  \ technological experiences, it aims to support more situated, inclusive and insightful\
  \ conversations.\n\nThis responds to the challenge that advanced technologies often\
  \ go unseen. They can be embedded in complex systems, and their implications and\
  \ importance go unrecognized, even by those who use these systems. The infrastructure\
  \ they require and the means through which they are deployed also vary across locations\
  \ and sectors.\n\nThe project discovered that each approach had strengths and weaknesses,\
  \ particularly on axes of accessibility and conversation depth, with a mixed modalities\
  \ approach more likely to foster a broad and inclusive consultation program. \n"

# Why is this project important to include in the case book?
why-is-this-project-important-to-include-in-the: "Our Consultation Framework proposes\
  \ a way to anchor conversations around participants' recent technological interactions\
  \ to avoid getting lost in abstraction. Indeed, discussions on frontier technologies,\
  \ such as AI, tend to be abstract. Questions like \u201Chow can AI be deployed in\
  \ an ethical manner?\u201D or \u201Chow is AI going to change the nature of work?\u201D\
  \ beg specification of what is meant by AI and who is likely to be impacted. To\
  \ make things more complicated, advanced technologies sometimes go unseen. They\
  \ can be embedded in complex systems, and their implications and importance go unrecognized,\
  \ even by those who use these systems. The infrastructure they require and the means\
  \ through which they are deployed also vary across locations and sectors.\n\nTo\
  \ address these challenges, our Framework offers a flexible foundation for structuring\
  \ consultations, rather than a prescriptive checklist of action items. The recommendations\
  \ it provides should be adapted for specific frontier technologies (like AI or bioengineering),\
  \ national contexts, or technological maturity, to ensure situated, inclusive and\
  \ insightful conversations. We hope that supporting these kinds of consultations\
  \ will create space for new dialogues and ultimately diversify inputs to global\
  \ conversations on these technologies."

# What limitations to the project should be noted?
what-limitations-to-the-project-should-be-noted: "**Diversity of methods was privileged\
  \ over representativity of results, as the idea was to refine our Framework.**\n\
  \n**From the authors' Project Report:**\n> \n> Our time in Morocco provided valuable\
  \ insight into hosting consultations and contributed to the strengthening of the\
  \ Framework and recommendations made.\n> \n> **Clear communications: **\n> \n> Close\
  \ collaboration and communication with partners are essential for the success of\
  \ consultations. This collaboration ensures alignment of objectives, shapes participants'\
  \ expectations, and allows for a more tailored approach to engagement. Partners'\
  \ deep understanding of the local context and community dynamics provides invaluable\
  \ insights for designing meaningful dialogues. Open communication fosters trust\
  \ and mutual respect, enabling us to navigate challenges and adapt our approach\
  \ as needed, ensuring that the consultations are relevant, engaging, and beneficial\
  \ for all involved parties.\n> \n> **Co-creation:**\n> \n> It would be beneficial\
  \ to foster closer collaboration with the local Accelerator Labs before the trip.\
  \ This would help allow a clearer understanding of the stakeholders involved in\
  \ the consultations, allowing for better preparation and tailored approaches. Furthermore,\
  \ allocating more time prior to the consultation for co-creation would be beneficial.\
  \ This proactive approach would enable facilitators to\n> anticipate potential challenges\
  \ in the dialogue's implementation, providing ample time to secure additional support\
  \ like translators or notetakers to assist during the consultations.\n> \n> **Working\
  \ with partners: **\n> \n> Good communication with partners about the purpose of\
  \ the consultation is essential, but not always sufficient during the preparatory\
  \ stages. Different entities have different interests and agendas. Working together\
  \ requires aligning objectives early on, meeting all partners\u2019 priorities in\
  \ the middle, and spending the necessary time on designing the agenda, messaging,\
  \ and tone of the consultation correctly. This consideration has been emphasized\
  \ in our Framework, focusing on effectively socializing the need for consultations\
  \ to avoid any priming effects that might influence participants\u2019 expectations\
  \ or responses.\n> \n> **Selecting consultation modalities: **\n> \n> Each consultation\
  \ modality has benefits and drawbacks, many of which are contingent on the number\
  \ of people and space limitations. We planned for focus groups which work best with\
  \ small groups of people who share similar backgrounds and knowledge levels. In\
  \ order to get enough participants, higher numbers of invitations were sent out.\
  \ We were fortunate to get higher than expected participation based on those invitations,\
  \ which resulted in rooms that had in the range of 15 to 30 people. These were effectively\
  \ closer to \u201Ctown halls\u201D than focus groups, which affected the dynamics\
  \ of the consultation. This highlighted the importance of tailoring consultation\
  \ methodology to the participants, and choosing the right space, both for quality\
  \ of responses as well as ease for the facilitator. New recommendations in the Framework\
  \ address the appropriate times to use different\n> modalities, and resources to\
  \ allow facilitators to adapt. \n> \n> **Managing participants: **\n> \n> During\
  \ consultations, facilitators have the difficult task of keeping the conversation\
  \ focused and balancing the voices in the room, particularly when stronger voices\
  \ threaten to overpower weaker ones. Facilitators should be aware of existing cultural\
  \ power\n> structures that have made it into the room and can influence who is given\
  \ more time and space to speak. In Morocco, gender as well as age dynamics were\
  \ clear in the consultation. For consultations to be inclusive, it\u2019s important\
  \ for all participants to be able to share freely. Smaller group sizes could be\
  \ more manageable for facilitators, allowing for better capture of sentiments and\
  \ more balanced participation. New recommendations in our Framework offer strategies\
  \ for facilitators to achieve this balance, both during the consultation and in\
  \ the preparatory phase. By intentionally forming smaller, more diverse groups,\
  \ we can create an environment where participants feel more comfortable sharing,\
  \ ensuring a richer and more inclusive dialogue.\n> \n> **Adjusting questions: **\n\
  > \n> These preliminary consultations allowed us to observe how questions were interpreted\
  \ by participants and pointed to where adjustments needed to be made. In some cases,\
  \ this meant adjusting wording to make questions more straightforward or to keep\
  \ the\n> sequence from feeling repetitive. Given that our consultations also took\
  \ place in up to three languages, sometimes all in the same conversation or even\
  \ sentence, our time in Morocco also highlighted the role that translation can play\
  \ in facilitating consultations. Expanding\n> the question list and adding notes\
  \ on intention allows facilitators more agency in selecting questions, ensuring\
  \ better flow of conversation, and mitigating confusion of participants. This approach\
  \ also allows for facilitators to probe deeper into responses offered, which helps\
  \ to mitigate priming effects of socializing or address mirrored responses from\
  \ participants based on what they have heard and not\n> first-hand experience.\n\
  > \n> **Travel considerations: **\n> \n> Travel and logistical considerations should\
  \ always be carefully planned, especially when aiming\n> to implement dialogues\
  \ in rural areas. In the case of Morocco, combining travel challenges with Ramadan\
  \ proved to be particularly difficult. For future consultations, it might be more\
  \ practical to plan our consultations in a way that does not disrupt the debriefing\
  \ process each day. This could involve scheduling shorter trips or ensuring accommodations\
  \ that allow for necessary daily reflections and adjustments.\n\n> **Methodology\
  \ amendments from the trip**\n> Over the course of the week, we revised the Framework\
  \ five times, incorporating insights generated during consultations that identified\n\
  > issues with the current version. The consultations revealed that the internal\
  \ logic of the mechanism to determine a person\u2019s familiarity with\n> AI worked\
  \ well. However, beginning a conversation with \u2018have you heard about AI?\u2019\
  \ was less effective in street consultations and focus groups with experts (two\
  \ extremes of knowledge about AI). While in the first case, respondents were not\
  \ able to provide an answer at all, in the second case respondents provided excessively\
  \ long answers, often out of the scope of this consultation. To set the context\
  \ for the following consultation in both cases, concrete examples of AI proxies\
  \ were introduced to participants. This turned out\n> to be an effective method\
  \ to correct a difficult start into a consultation.\n\n> **Reflections**\n> \n>\
  \ This section is primarily borne out of our experience during the piloting\n> phase\
  \ in Morocco and Paraguay.\n> \n> **Socializing the need for a consultation**\n\
  > \n> Given the rapid advancement and deployment of technology in recent years,\
  \ for many it will seem natural that these consultations be\n> conducted. Others\
  \ may have questions about the purpose of them, how meaningful they will be able\
  \ to be, or even whether there is a need for them in the first place. Questions\
  \ and hesitancies may come from local UN staff, governments, partner organizations,\
  \ or participants. The\n> effectiveness of consultations, and quality of the data\
  \ collected, may be impacted without buy-in from everyone involved. This makes socializing\
  \ the consultations a crucial step in a successful consultation process.\n> \n>\
  \ As we\u2019ve outlined in sections above, meaningful conversation on our digital\
  \ future has failed to reach everyone who stands to be impacted.\n> This has left\
  \ far too many people around the world without a voice. But even without a voice,\
  \ those individuals stand to lose or gain from\n> decisions made at key part of\
  \ the tech development and regulation cycle, including decisions regarding data\
  \ governance and privacy protections, environmental concerns, ethical guardrails,\
  \ language functionality, and meeting user needs.\n> \n> Wording used in invitations\
  \ and information campaigns before consultations have the potential to prime the\
  \ consultation process in the\n> participants minds, influencing the answers that\
  \ are given. This means that care should be taken to keep all language about the\
  \ consultation as tech-agnostic as possible, from start to finish. By being clear\
  \ and unbiased in the language around consultations, expectations will be clear\
  \ on both sides and the responses received will be as robust and authentic as possible.\n\
  > \n> In order to engage a wide-reaching and diverse audience, it may also be necessary\
  \ to work with a number of external partners in civil society, government, or the\
  \ private sector. These partners may have their own perspectives and embedded ideas\
  \ about frontier technologies, which may come across during consultation, or during\
  \ the socialization process.\n> \n> This may come in the form of government partners\
  \ who have active wide-reaching technology initiatives in progress, or from private\
  \ sector\n> partners in the tech industry that are interested in focusing on the\
  \ positive messages about their products. This has the potential to bias\n> participant\
  \ responses which compromises the data collection process and any potential findings.\n\
  > \n> While it is understandable that facilitators would not want to compromise\
  \ important partnerships, they also have a moral and ethical obligation to both\
  \ participants and the rest of the UN facilitators to keep data collection as unbiased\
  \ as possible. Whenever possible, unbiased partners should be prioritized. When\
  \ working with biased partners is unavoidable, the potential bias should be noted\
  \ in data collection. When entering into any partnership, UN consultation implementers\
  \ should engage in intentional conversation with partners about how the consultations\
  \ should be marketed to participants.\n> \n> Participants may also come in with\
  \ strong ideas about tech, both positive or negative, that they have picked up from\
  \ media, friends, or other\n> sources. While these perceptions are important to\
  \ hear, they are also important to interrogate during consultations. Before consultations,\
  \ it is important to be aware of the political landscape around tech in the country,\
  \ including what narratives participants are likely to have heard or picked up in\
  \ the country. This can include doing topic analysis on social media or news reporting\
  \ before the consultations. By doing so,\n> facilitators are able to prepare questions\
  \ that ask participants to go deeper into specific areas that are likely to be mentioned.\
  \ This will ensure that consultations go beyond surface level and produce meaningful\
  \ sentiment data that are reflective of each individual participant\u2019s\n> perspectives.\n\
  > \n> It\u2019s important to note that while the UN plays a crucial role in these\
  \ conversations, the organization itself has a distinct positionality. Hence,\n\
  > participants of consultations may feel obligated to engage or give specific \u201C\
  correct\u201D answers which they believe the UN would like to hear. Acknowledging\
  \ the potential biases in responses and emphasizing the voluntary nature of the\
  \ consultations can help alleviate this, as can encouraging participants to ask\
  \ questions before or after the consultation about their purpose.\n"

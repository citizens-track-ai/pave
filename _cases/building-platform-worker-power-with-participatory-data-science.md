# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: "Since 2024, the Workers\u2019 Observatory\
  \ (WO) has involved food delivery riders in participatory research that explored\
  \ marginalised workers\u2019 experiences of AI-driven management, and turned it\
  \ into data and evidence that has informed decision-making in and beyond the city.\
  \ \n\nIt has forged a Scottish agenda, leading Edinburgh City Council to put improving\
  \ gig work conditions at the heart of its Fair Work action plan. It has also expanded\
  \ the frontiers of participatory worker research internationally, including workshops\
  \ in Chennai and Kolkata. It has led to the featuring of workers  in Human Rights\
  \ Watch\u2019s \u2018Algorithms of Exploitation\u2019 thereby elevating the international\
  \ visibility of AI-driven algorithmic mismanagement in the platform economy, ahead\
  \ of the ILO\u2019s adoption of the Decent Work in the Platform Economy Convention.\
  \ \n\nIn doing so it has pioneered Participatory Worker Data Science, a method that\
  \ empowers workers to generate robust data-driven evidence about locally identified\
  \ harms, providing them with powerful leverage for shaping local policy debates\
  \ for themselves. The method was developed through experiments involving platform\
  \ workers in data-mapping, data parsing, and forming data-driven rates demands.\
  \ Outputs increased workers\u2019 influence in policy debates about harms associated\
  \ with AI-driven work; our findings persuaded Edinburgh City Council to address\
  \ unfair gig work and elevate the issue to Scottish Government level.\n\n**Benefits\
  \ of a worker-led methodology**\n\nOur processes relied used a participatory methodology\
  \ we call \u2018worker data science\u2019 (WDS). This method centres local context\
  \ and concerns, building on radical citizen science, workers\u2019 inquiry, and\
  \ participatory action research. WDS involves platform or other on-demand workers\
  \ surfacing research questions about the organisation or process of work (e.g. do\
  \ deactivation rates increase for undocumented migrants; what risks deter women\
  \ from working?) and, supported by social scientists, in generating evidence and\
  \ answers to these questions.\_\n\nWorkers are often well-placed and motivated to\
  \ answer their own questions with data they can collect, such as pay slips or platform\
  \ data and then are often amenable to generating new forms of data collectively\
  \ (such as through worker-led surveys or field notes) or using new research tools\
  \ (such a worker-led projects like RooParse, which enabled riders to track their\
  \ wages over time). Workers can then share methods and data-validated knowledge\
  \ across locations, empowering them to advocate and articulate the rights they seek.\
  \ \u2018Popular\u2019 knowledge enters dialogue with \u2018academic\u2019 knowledge,\
  \ and communities are empowered to mitigate risks of technological change.\n\nWe\
  \ can illustrate how this looked in a workshop in Edinburgh. More than 20 workers\
  \ engaged in over two days during which WO facilitated collective inquiry by workers\
  \ of the way that AI-driven systems like dynamic pricing were affecting them. Through\
  \ exercises with other organisations including Worker Info Exchange and the Independent\
  \ Workers of Great Britain, participants undertook exercises to explore how AI-driven\
  \ technologies affect them. Workers mapped the power imbalance between platform\
  \ companies and couriers and the ability of the former to gather and process huge\
  \ amounts of data to drive dynamic pricing, tailor fees, and pay sub-minimum wages\
  \ and unequal rates for equal work. Based on these exercises, workers formed precise\
  \ demands and a strategy for non-cooperation with a process \u2013 \u2018add-on\u2019\
  \ orders \u2013 felt to be most exploitative and inequitable. This demand became\
  \ the basis for a video and flyer created by participants to build awareness among\
  \ other riders and the public. Participants investigated companies\u2019 concealment\
  \ of information and algorithms from riders, including data to which workers are\
  \ legally entitled. They connected this to frequent blocking of accounts and de-activation,\
  \ higher rate of blocking and longer sign-on time for South Asian people, and app-blocking\
  \ for those who share data with third-party apps (preventing workers from pooling\
  \ and sharing their own data). They learnt ways (e.g. Subject Access Requests) to\
  \ reclaim and use data to challenge cases where people are paid unequally for the\
  \ same order. Workers discussed tools used elsewhere in the world and debated whether\
  \ to develop their own data-driven tools, e.g. to share real-time data allowing\
  \ them to see fees platforms are paying and inform decisions when accepting orders\
  \ or deciding when to work.\n"

# Why is this project important to include in the case book?
why-is-this-project-important-to-include-in-the: "As AI-driven labour platforms proliferate\
  \ in sectors from food delivery and ridehailing to care and nursing, workers, NGO\
  \ and researchers lack the data, methods and conceptual tools to understand workers\u2019\
  \ conditions and how they can be improved in local contexts. High level debates\
  \ are mostly uninformed by granular local evidence, while workers facing myriad\
  \ harms across countries have unequal access to data to evidence and rights to mitigate\
  \ them. The Workers' Observatory has addressed this problem through pioneering methods\
  \ for data and evidence generated with and by platform workers to be mobilised to\
  \ build their public voice and political power. By exercising data rights, and collecting\
  \ personal data, experiences, and knowledge, platform workers generated evidence\
  \ otherwise impossible to obtain. Using this evidence to engage at city, country,\
  \ and cross-continental level, the findings have been used to articulate a set of\
  \ standards and advocate for a new Fair Gig Work charter at city level; and to advocate\
  \ for improvements to health and safety rights internationally. \n\nThese participatory\
  \ methods provided environments where marginalised workers working under digital\
  \ platforms were able to come together and form communities. During one workshop,\
  \ worker participants formed ROOM, a riders\u2019 collective, that is now a city\
  \ stakeholder. As the quotes below demonstrate, starting from a process of research\
  \ and knowledge-sharing creates an environment of collective confidence, collaboration\
  \ and initiative that has given voice to some of the workers most impacted by AI-driven\
  \ exploitation. In coalition with 2 other majority-migrant organisations, WO has\
  \ established Migrant Justice Edinburgh, which has received \xA31 million of funding\
  \ over 10 years to elevate the voices and concerns of migrant workers including\
  \ those in the AI-driven gig economy. \n"

# Inclusion efforts
inclusion-efforts: "Recruitment and engagement were carried out by the Workers' Observatory,\
  \ a charity and research collaboration that involved food delivery riders and other\
  \ marginalised AI-driven platform workers in research projects to support advocacy\
  \ efforts for fairer, safer conditions. Its' methods include using Participatory\
  \ Worker Data Science (PWDS) to empower workers to generate robust, data-driven\
  \ evidence about locally identified harms, to provide them with powerful leverage\
  \ for shaping local, national and international rights debates.\n\nThe research\
  \ takes place through in-person workshops, before which the WO project officer in\
  \ Edinburgh - and organisers of the partner organisation SAFAR in Kolkata - spent\
  \ time engaging one-to-one on the streets with workers, particularly those from\
  \ marginalised migrant communities. Trusted relationships were built by a paid organiser\
  \ through meeting in caf\xE9s and talking during shifts, and developed through building\
  \ social bonds in pubs, accepting invitations to meals at riders\u2019 homes, and\
  \ providing personal support during crises. This led workers to commit to attending\
  \ sessions, and some trusted workers served as translators and interpreters, ensuring\
  \ peer-level support so other workers felt comfortable.\n"

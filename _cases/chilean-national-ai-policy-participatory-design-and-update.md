# Headline findings
headline-findings: "Regional participants said AI should respond to territorial needs.\
  \ In the Aysen regional roundtable, participants called for \u201CLlevar la IA a\
  \ la resoluci\xF3n de problemas relacionados al territorio\u201D [bringing AI to\
  \ solve territory-specific problems], including local production, agriculture, livestock,\
  \ regional universities and public services.\n\nParticipants said privacy, consent\
  \ and data governance must be central. Regional participants asked: \u201C\xBFQui\xE9\
  n resguarda la privacidad y el consentimiento de uso de los datos?\u201D [Who safeguards\
  \ privacy and consent for data use?]. They also proposed \u201CProtocolos de uso\
  \ de datos (consentimientos informados)\u201D [data-use protocols and informed consent].\n\
  \nParticipants said AI policy requires public literacy, not only expert capacity.\
  \ Regional participants called for \u201CAlfabetizaci\xF3n en IA\u201D [AI literacy],\
  \ \u201CCursos de alfabetizaci\xF3n digital obligatorio en Educaci\xF3n Escolar\u201D\
  \ [mandatory digital literacy courses in school education], and \u201CIntroducir\
  \ temas vinculados a IA a la poblaci\xF3n general\u201D [introducing AI topics to\
  \ the general population].\n\nParticipants said AI should improve work rather than\
  \ simply replace workers. In regional discussions, participants said the policy\
  \ discourse should incorporate \u201Cla mejora y no p\xE9rdida de trabajo\u201D\
  \ [improvement and not job loss]. A public consultation respondent put it more sharply:\
  \ \u201Cpersonas antes que m\xE1quinas\u201D [people before machines].\n\nParticipants\
  \ said Chile should build human capabilities alongside infrastructure. Consultation\
  \ responses emphasized education, training, talent development and reskilling as\
  \ core conditions for responsible AI adoption, rather than focusing only on technological\
  \ infrastructure.\n\nRAM participants saw AI as an opportunity for better public\
  \ policy, but warned about democratic risks. In the 2023 RAM discussions, participants\
  \ identified opportunities for AI to improve public services, broaden participation\
  \ and detect corruption, while also raising concerns about misinformation, surveillance,\
  \ loss of autonomy, opacity and explainability.\n\nRAM participants said AI in health\
  \ and education should complement human roles. Participants saw opportunities for\
  \ personalized health and education and more efficient public services, but raised\
  \ concerns about empathy, mental health, local bias, rural connectivity gaps and\
  \ overreliance on automated systems.\n\n2024 consultation respondents asked for\
  \ stronger governance, regulation and accountability. Their comments pushed the\
  \ updated policy to strengthen transparency, accountability, audits, protection\
  \ of personal data, human rights impact assessment, procurement standards and citizen\
  \ participation in AI regulation.\n\n2024 consultation respondents said inclusion\
  \ should go beyond gender alone. Comments supported gender equality but also pushed\
  \ for a broader focus on inclusion and non-discrimination, including bias in datasets,\
  \ unequal access to technology, and participation of diverse social groups in AI\
  \ research and development.\n\n2024 consultation respondents raised new concerns\
  \ linked to generative AI. They highlighted children and adolescents, intellectual\
  \ property, culture and heritage, cybersecurity, disinformation, digital violence\
  \ and safe digital ecosystems as issues needing stronger treatment in the updated\
  \ policy.\n"

# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: "Chile\u2019s National Artificial Intelligence\
  \ Policy is a ten-year strategy to guide the responsible development, adoption and\
  \ governance of AI in the country. Its original 2019-2021 development process began\
  \ as a more expert-led policy exercise but evolved into a broad participatory process\
  \ after Chile\u2019s 2019 social unrest and the COVID-19 pandemic. The Ministry\
  \ of Science, Technology, Knowledge and Innovation opened the process through regional\
  \ roundtables, self-organized roundtables, webinars and a public consultation, involving\
  \ thousands of participants from academia, industry, civil society, the public sector\
  \ and the general public.\n\nThe policy was later updated through a second participatory\
  \ process in 2023-2024, informed by UNESCO\u2019s Readiness Assessment Methodology,\
  \ regional thematic discussions, virtual expert sessions and a final online public\
  \ consultation. This update focused especially on governance, ethics, regulation,\
  \ labor impacts, inclusion, children and adolescents, intellectual property, culture,\
  \ cybersecurity, and environmental sustainability. Together, both phases show how\
  \ Chile used participatory methods to move AI governance beyond expert consultation\
  \ and incorporate territorial, social and ethical concerns into national AI policy.\n"

# Why is this project important to include in the case book?
why-is-this-project-important-to-include-in-the: "This project is important because\
  \ it shows how a national AI policy in Latin America moved beyond expert consultation\
  \ and created multiple channels for public, territorial and multi-sector participation.\
  \ The process deliberately treated non-expert knowledge as relevant to AI governance:\
  \ people\u2019s fears, expectations, territorial priorities, labor concerns and\
  \ social values were considered alongside technical expertise.\n\nThe case is also\
  \ distinctive because participation was sustained across two policy cycles. The\
  \ original 2019-2021 process included self-organized roundtables, regional roundtables,\
  \ webinars and a public consultation, adapting rapidly to COVID-19. The 2023-2024\
  \ update then used UNESCO\u2019s RAM process, regional thematic discussions, virtual\
  \ expert sessions and a final online public consultation to update the policy\u2019\
  s governance and ethics agenda.\n\nIt is especially relevant for the case book because\
  \ it illustrates participation under real institutional and political constraints:\
  \ social unrest, pandemic disruption, expert resistance, limited resources and a\
  \ change of government. Despite these challenges, the process helped make the policy\
  \ more legitimate and resilient, allowing it to survive political transition and\
  \ evolve into a policy of State."

# Inclusion efforts
inclusion-efforts: "The process made inclusion a central design principle across both\
  \ phases: the original 2019-2020 policy process and the 2023-2024 update. In the\
  \ first phase, the Ministry deliberately opened participation beyond technical experts,\
  \ inviting individuals, academia, industry, civil society, public-sector actors\
  \ and self-organized groups to contribute. This was especially important because\
  \ some actors initially argued that AI policy should be left mainly to experts.\
  \ The Ministry took the opposite position: social concerns, territorial priorities,\
  \ fears, expectations and lived experience were treated as legitimate inputs for\
  \ AI governance.\n\nSeveral mechanisms were used to lower barriers to participation.\
  \ The 2019-2020 process included self-organized roundtables, allowing groups to\
  \ convene their own discussions and submit inputs under common guidelines. The Ministry\
  \ supported them with guiding questions, optional presentations by public servants,\
  \ contact points, and a sponsorship mechanism that gave official recognition to\
  \ independently organized discussions. When COVID-19 disrupted in-person engagement,\
  \ the process moved online, deadlines were extended, and a participation manual\
  \ was prepared to help people organize virtual roundtables.\n\nThe process also\
  \ included Ministry-led regional roundtables and webinars. The regional roundtables\
  \ brought territorial perspectives into a policy area often concentrated in Santiago\
  \ and expert communities, while the webinars helped build a shared baseline of understanding\
  \ about AI for people without technical backgrounds.\n\nThe update process continued\
  \ and expanded this inclusive approach. The RAM process held six thematic discussions\
  \ in different regions, with participants from academia, industry, the public sector\
  \ and civil society, plus virtual expert sessions. These discussions used backcasting\
  \ methods to help participants imagine possible AI futures for Chile and identify\
  \ actions needed today. The final 2024 online consultation was open to individuals,\
  \ legal entities and groups, promoted through the Ministry\u2019s website and social\
  \ media, and received input from all regions of Chile.\n\nNo peer-researcher model\
  \ or multilingual translation process was used. Inclusion relied mainly on open\
  \ access, regional outreach, self-organized participation, hybrid and online formats,\
  \ public dissemination, structured guidance, and explicit recognition of non-expert\
  \ knowledge.\n"
# What limitations to the project should be noted?
what-limitations-to-the-project-should-be-noted: "Several limitations should be noted.\
  \ First, participation was broad but not statistically representative of Chile\u2019\
  s population. Many activities relied on open calls, institutional networks, stakeholder\
  \ organizations and self-selection, which likely favored people and groups already\
  \ interested in AI, technology, public policy or digital rights.\n\nSecond, the\
  \ process was affected by major external disruptions. The original 2019-2020 process\
  \ took place during the aftermath of Chile\u2019s social unrest and then the COVID-19\
  \ pandemic. COVID-19 forced activities to move online, which allowed the process\
  \ to continue and even expand geographically, but may have excluded people with\
  \ limited connectivity, digital skills, time or access to online participation tools.\n\
  \nThird, participation was uneven across groups. Expert, professional and highly\
  \ educated participants were strongly represented, particularly in the public consultation\
  \ stages. Gender participation was also uneven in some parts of the process, with\
  \ men overrepresented in the 2021 consultation and the 2024 consultation.\n\nFourth,\
  \ although regional roundtables and the RAM discussions helped include territorial\
  \ perspectives, the process did not use targeted recruitment methods for specific\
  \ marginalized communities, peer researchers, or multilingual facilitation. Indigenous\
  \ peoples, rural communities, children and adolescents, people with disabilities,\
  \ and other groups affected by AI were addressed in the policy discussion, but not\
  \ always engaged through tailored methods.\n\nFinally, there are limits in accountability\
  \ and traceability. The consultations and RAM process produced reports and influenced\
  \ the policy update, but not every contribution can be traced directly to a specific\
  \ change in the final text. Policy drafting still involved synthesis by the Ministry,\
  \ expert input and administrative decision-making.\n"

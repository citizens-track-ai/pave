# Headline findings
headline-findings: "The current AI governance landscape is dominated by a narrow set\
  \ of state and corporate actors, even though AI's impacts are whole-of-society by\
  \ nature and by design. Exacerbating this is the evidence gathered across our stakeholder\
  \ groups that the impact of consultative processes and grassroots participatory\
  \ approaches is limited by the absence of common frameworks and tools, transparency\
  \ and follow-through, and openness to more diverse actors.\n\nAI governance for\
  \ democracy needs to be a broad-participation and well-networked approach, inclusive\
  \ of a wide range of participants, like citizens and CSOs, that are currently underrepresented,\
  \ alongside more \u201Ctraditional\u201D agents of change, within a model that effectively\
  \ and actively balances power as a function of its design and mandate. \n\nA viable\
  \ governance model that meaningfully incorporates citizen and civil society inputs\
  \ must have a strong, legally backed mandate, well-framed outcome-oriented issues,\
  \ practical solutions, modulated power dynamics, and procedural openness and visibility.\
  \ \n\nThe involvement of citizens in co-regulation, the moderation of private sector\
  \ behaviour by up and downstream actor influence, and the integration of citizen\
  \ wisdom are key requirements for generating meaningful change in the way AI is\
  \ governed in the future.  \n\nImportantly, CSOs\u2019 readiness for participating\
  \ in AI governance remains low, driven by a self-reinforcing exclusion narrative,\
  \ discouraging proactive efforts towards the very engagement, capacity-building,\
  \ and awareness-raising that would earn them a seat at the table. \n\nKey barriers\
  \ to effective CSO agenda-setting are: deployment of AI in public services without\
  \ transparency and accountability; barriers limiting access to information about\
  \ AI use; ethical risks from poor data governance; perception of civil society as\
  \ blockers that sidelines deliberation in favour of moving fast; and fragmentation\
  \ of CSO efforts lacking shared infrastructure to coordinate and strategise together.\n\
  \nKey enablers of more effective CSO engagement include: mass awareness and public\
  \ mobilisation through simple, relatable narratives at scale; strategic capacity-building\
  \ to navigate informal decision-making spaces and build alliances; cross-sector\
  \ coalitions to boost credibility and reach; shift from critique to offering practical\
  \ alternatives and proven methods; stronger, aligned messaging grounded in lived\
  \ experience; and early engagement in policy cycles with concrete recommendations.\n"

# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: "AI governance for democracy demands a\
  \ multistakeholder, well-networked framework. This was the finding of our Collaborative\
  \ AI Governance for Tomorrow\u2019s Democracies project, which consist of three\
  \ phases - deep issue exploration, participatory engagement, and dissemination and\
  \ practical implementation. Our approach is collaborative, participatory, and co-creative\
  \ by design, uniting people with experience, power, and expertise to co-create a\
  \ framework and insights for systemic change that deliver real impact. What surfaces\
  \ is a new understanding of what needs to be true to create change, shining a particular\
  \ spotlight on and giving voice to the lived experience of CSOs across the globe.\n\
  \nThe project\u2019s impact was delivered through a systematic review of evidence\
  \ of the AI governance landscape, furthered through a qualitative study with representatives\
  \ from civil society, regulatory and public bodies, research institutes and academics,\
  \ tech companies, and the wider private sector. In all, we consulted more than 50\
  \ stakeholders to co-create an approach to increasing citizens' role in governance,\
  \ understanding and overcoming the barriers to engagement, and exploring effective\
  \ models for inclusion. \n\nThe outcome of the project is three-fold. Firstly, we\
  \ delivered a first-of-its-kind framework for collaborative AI governance, which\
  \ we have called an AI Issue Association, that makes space for effective participation\
  \ of citizens and civil society. Our findings show that to do this, a strong mandate\
  \ with enforcement teeth, granular and well-scoped problem definitions, practical\
  \ commitments, and balanced power dynamics through inclusive, depoliticised structures\
  \ are all prerequisites. Only when these elements are in place can citizen participation\
  \ move from consultation to governance. Our project\u2019s second phase - the Power\
  \ to Shape AI CSO Conversation, co-created a systematised set of needs, barriers,\
  \ and enablers for civil society to fulfil its role as agenda-setters, wielding\
  \ effective influence over AI governance. And thirdly, we built and tapped into\
  \ existing networks of civil society organisations, academics, and AI governing\
  \ regulatory bodies, to apply the findings in practice. Our ongoing work now focuses\
  \ on deploying a citizen engagement on the role of AI in hindering and improving\
  \ democratic processes as well as operationalising the framework through our coalition-building\
  \ activities.\n"

# Why is this project important to include in the case book?
why-is-this-project-important-to-include-in-the: "Questions around the human-centric\
  \ governance of AI are inseparable from the wider socio-technical polycrisis that\
  \ defines our current reality. Trust is declining and polarisation is growing. Meanwhile,\
  \ regulation remains fragmented, abstract, and insufficient to the scale of the\
  \ challenge. The current approach to AI governance predominantly focuses on states\
  \ and Big Tech as the two key stakeholder groups afforded leverage and decision-making\
  \ power. However, AI is a technology that impacts the whole of society. Its implications\
  \ extend far beyond governments and Big Tech alone.\n\nThe initiative advances broad-participation\
  \ governance frameworks that place citizens and civil society organisations (CSOs)\
  \ on an equal footing in the AI governance ecosystem. The case for participatory\
  \ AI rests on both principle and pragmatism. On principle, it stems from a commitment\
  \ to democracy, human rights, and autonomy. At the same time, participation is a\
  \ strategic move to align technical development with a robustly discerned public\
  \ interest, and to build the mechanisms and mobilised publics that can hold AI's\
  \ developers and governance bodies accountable.\n\nMost critically, this project\
  \ addresses a practical gap. Meaningful public involvement is needed at every level,\
  \ but participation can't happen everywhere at once. We need clear processes, practical\
  \ tools, shared approaches, and common language so that efforts on the ground and\
  \ efforts at scale align and multiply their impact. To underpin this all, we also\
  \ need a robust understanding, grounded in lived experience, of the current state\
  \ of CSO influence over AI governance, the barriers to effective agenda-setting,\
  \ and their needs and enablers to overcome these barriers. Our project delivered\
  \ on this need.\n\nOur approach has already gathered support and resonance from\
  \ academia, civil society, and global institutions, as we have started building\
  \ a community of practice around a more robust framework for AI governance with\
  \ a focus on civic voices and upholding human rights. The results of this project\
  \ have been shared with UNESCO\u2019s Global Civil Society Organizations and Academic\
  \ Network on AI Ethics and Policy, presented at leading international conferences\
  \ and events, including the Paris Conference on AI and Digital Ethics, the PAIRS:\
  \ Participatory AI and Research Practice Symposium, the Vilnius University International\
  \ Scientific Conference: Human Centric AI: Ethics, Regulation, and Safety, AI Dialogues,\
  \ and informally shared at the 2025 UN AI for Good Summit. Including this project\
  \ in the PAVE Casebook would further improve the visibility and potential uptake\
  \ of these valuable insights and framework for systemic change."

# Inclusion efforts
inclusion-efforts: 'Efforts were made to ensure the recruitment and engagement process
  was accessible and inclusive. We designed a flexible process that respected participants''
  other life and professional commitments, recognising that many CSO representatives,
  academics, and public sector stakeholders operate under significant time constraints.
  One-on-one interviews were conducted online and scheduled around participants''
  availability, lowering barriers to entry and removing the need for travel. Online
  co-creation on our participatory platform, Clever Together Conversations, was asynchronous,
  allowing participants to contribute at their own leisure. Two virtual workshops
  were also held to accommodate participants across different time zones. We sought
  to accommodate individual accessibility needs, adapting the format and approach
  to ensure that participation was as open and equitable as possible.

  '
# What limitations to the project should be noted?
what-limitations-to-the-project-should-be-noted: 'The project constitutes a framework
  intended to provide evidence-based grounding for further action, rather than a completed
  intervention. Its ultimate impact depends on large-scale coalition-building efforts
  and on the framework being actively taken forward by partners across sectors. This
  is a systemic change initiative for a wicked problem with no straightforward solution.
  In fact, one of the biggest findings of the framework creation phase was the need
  for people to take radical personal responsibility to influence change in the areas
  where they hold power. The value demonstrated to date is foundational, laying the
  groundwork for collective action that has yet to be fully realised at scale.


  Sustaining and expanding this work requires dedicated funding and committed partnerships.
  To address this, we have applied for funding through the EU-funded ScaleDem project
  for a first-of-its kind citizen participatory engagement on democratic bias in AI
  in Bulgaria and are pursuing a partnership with Make.org to support a larger-scale
  engagement effort, alongside broader coalition-building efforts across our expanding
  partnership network on human-centric AI (50+ partners to date). Direct citizen engagement
  represents the critical next phase of the project, and securing the funding necessary
  to deliver it remains an active priority.

  '

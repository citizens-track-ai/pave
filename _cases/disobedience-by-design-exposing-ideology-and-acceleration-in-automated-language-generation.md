# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: "Disobedience by Design: Exposing Ideology\
  \ and Acceleration in Automated Language Generation is a practice-based doctoral\
  \ research project investigating how public and artistic engagement can reveal the\
  \ power dynamics embedded in Large Language Models (LLMs) and generative AI systems.\
  \ Developed within the field of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), the project combines\
  \ critical design, AI-art practice, participatory workshops, and public exhibitions\
  \ to explore how AI-mediated language production shapes creativity, labour, communication,\
  \ and social relations.\n\nThe research emerged from concerns regarding the accelerating\
  \ adoption of generative AI systems in creative and cultural sectors. While LLMs\
  \ are frequently presented as neutral productivity tools, the project examines how\
  \ they also embody ideological assumptions related to efficiency, optimisation,\
  \ automation, and economic extraction. In particular, the research investigates\
  \ how AI systems may reinforce existing power asymmetries between technology corporations,\
  \ workers, artists, and audiences, while contributing to cultural homogenisation\
  \ and the marginalisation of alternative perspectives.\n\nTo investigate these issues,\
  \ the project developed two interactive AI artworks called Isotta and Alessia, collectively\
  \ framed as Disobedient Discursive Machines. Rather than celebrating technological\
  \ innovation, these systems were intentionally designed to resist dominant narratives\
  \ surrounding AI progress.\n\nIsotta combines a mechanical typewriter with a Large\
  \ Language Model connected to the social platform X/Twitter. While participants\
  \ slowly type text using the analogue machine, the AI periodically interrupts the\
  \ writing process by autonomously generating unexpected continuations. The interaction\
  \ intentionally creates tension and discomfort between human agency and machine\
  \ automation, exposing the asymmetry of power introduced by AI-assisted communication.\n\
  \nAlessia is a \u201Clazy\u201D AI voice assistant built using an obsolete reel-to-reel\
  \ tape recorder. Unlike commercial voice assistants designed around immediacy and\
  \ frictionless interaction, Alessia responds slowly, mechanically, and imperfectly.\
  \ The system rewinds tapes between utterances and forces participants to wait, listen,\
  \ and reflect on the temporal assumptions embedded in contemporary AI systems. Through\
  \ this deliberate slowness, the artwork invites audiences to critically reconsider\
  \ the relationship between automation, productivity, and human experience.\n\nThe\
  \ project engaged diverse publics through exhibitions, workshops, focus groups,\
  \ and participatory creative activities. The artworks were exhibited in artistic\
  \ and cultural venues, including international digital art contexts, where audiences\
  \ interacted directly with the systems and collectively discussed their experiences.\
  \ Additional workshops involved artists experimenting with LLMs for creative writing\
  \ and speculative storytelling activities. These engagements created spaces where\
  \ participants could articulate concerns about AI, including loss of creative agency,\
  \ dependency on proprietary platforms, bias, opacity, labour exploitation, and the\
  \ pressure toward accelerated production.\n\nThe research adopted qualitative and\
  \ participatory methodologies, including field observations, interviews, thematic\
  \ analysis, abductive analysis, and semiotic interpretation. Artistic practice was\
  \ used not only as a method for producing artefacts, but also as a situated form\
  \ of inquiry capable of generating critical reflection and public discussion around\
  \ AI systems. Participants were not treated as passive audiences but as co-interpreters\
  \ whose experiences, interpretations, and critiques informed the development of\
  \ the research outcomes.\n\nThe project contributed three main outcomes relevant\
  \ to public engagement on AI. First, it demonstrated how artistic and embodied interaction\
  \ can make abstract AI power dynamics tangible and discussable for broader publics.\
  \ Second, it developed design tactics for critical engagement with AI, including\
  \ the use of obsolete technologies as political design materials, the contestation\
  \ of technological speed and efficiency, and the introduction of friction as a mechanism\
  \ for reflection. Third, it proposed analytical frameworks for understanding how\
  \ ideological assumptions become embedded in AI-generated language and user interaction.\n\
  \nMore broadly, the project argues that meaningful public engagement with AI should\
  \ move beyond simplified narratives of innovation and risk, and instead create opportunities\
  \ for situated, critical, and participatory reflection on the social values embedded\
  \ in AI systems. By combining artistic practice, participatory engagement, and critical\
  \ HCI research, Disobedience by Design explores how public voices and creative communities\
  \ can contribute to more democratic, power-aware, and socially accountable discussions\
  \ about the future of AI.\n"

# Why is this project important to include in the case book?
why-is-this-project-important-to-include-in-the: ' This project is important to include
  in the PAVE Case Book because it demonstrates how artistic and participatory methods
  can support critical public engagement with generative AI beyond conventional awareness
  or consultation models. Rather than focusing only on technical literacy or policy
  discussion, the project created embodied and reflective experiences that helped
  participants critically examine the social, cultural, and political dimensions of
  AI systems.


  A distinctive aspect of the project is its engagement with communities often marginal
  within AI governance discussions, particularly artists and cultural practitioners.
  The research foregrounded the experiences of people whose creative labour and cultural
  production are increasingly shaped by AI technologies, enabling participants to
  discuss issues such as automation, authorship, bias, opacity, labour exploitation,
  and loss of creative agency.


  The project also introduced novel engagement methods through interactive AI artworks
  built with obsolete technologies, including a mechanical typewriter and a reel-to-reel
  tape recorder connected to Large Language Models. Unlike commercial AI systems designed
  around speed and seamless interaction, these artworks intentionally introduced slowness,
  friction, interruption, and unpredictability. This design strategy transformed abstract
  AI infrastructures into tangible and experiential encounters, encouraging audiences
  to reflect on the values embedded in contemporary AI systems, particularly acceleration,
  efficiency, and technological control.


  Through exhibitions, workshops, interviews, and participatory activities, the project
  created spaces where audiences could collectively interpret and question AI technologies.
  Artistic practice functioned both as a public engagement method and as a research
  methodology capable of generating situated insight into how people experience and
  negotiate AI-mediated communication.


  The project is also relevant because it demonstrates how artistic and cultural settings
  can broaden participation in AI discussions beyond technical and institutional communities.
  By fostering open-ended interpretation, emotional engagement, and critical imagination,
  the research supported more inclusive and socially grounded conversations about
  the future of AI.


  Overall, the project offers a transferable example of how participatory art and
  critical design can contribute to democratic, reflective, and power-aware forms
  of public engagement with AI.'

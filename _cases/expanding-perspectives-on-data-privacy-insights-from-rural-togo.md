# Headline findings
headline-findings: "Experiential experts living in rural Togo surfaced a different\
  \ set of privacy concerns than those commonly raised by privacy and development\
  \ domain experts. Rather than a concern abut (mis)use by institutions (e.g., consent,\
  \ surveillance, re-identification), participants raised concern about (mis)use by\
  \ people in their community (e.g., shame, jealousy, limited autonomy).\n\nParticipant\
  \ quote. We had just used sociotechnical visuals to describe the data recorded in\
  \ mobile phone data. We asked the participant, a man in his 30s, how sensitive the\
  \ data was. He immediately thought about what would happen if the data was disclosued\
  \ to people in his community. He said: \n\n\u201Cthe one who gives [mobile money]\
  \ can be harmed if it is known in the community because it is known that he has\
  \ money and gives it to others. Some may be jealous of him. But the one receiving\
  \ may feel some kind of shame: people in the community will have a bad opinion of\
  \ him because he depends on other people to survive\"\n\nThen, we asked the participant\
  \ how he would feel if the government of Togo had access to the very same data.\
  \ He said, \"I won\u2019t be disturbed if the Togo government knows about [mobile\
  \ money balances and transactions]. They know that you are helping others. But among\
  \ ourselves here, it may be disturbing\u2026the government could know [because]\
  \ they can\u2019t envy me\u201D \n\nThis quote is emblematic of many of the people\
  \ we interviewed. While privacy and development domain experts tend to focus on\
  \ privacy harms that can arise when data is disclosed to other people within their\
  \ community. Rather than argue one is right and the other is wrong, we demonstrate\
  \ that the privacy concerns of domain experts and experiential experts are both\
  \ important. \n"

# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: 'Passively collected "big" data sources
  are increasingly used to inform critical development policy decisions in low- and
  middle-income countries. While prior work highlights how such approaches may reveal
  sensitive information, enable surveillance, and centralize power, less is known
  about the corresponding privacy concerns, hopes, and fears of the people directly
  impacted by these policies -- people sometimes referred to as experiential experts.
  To understand the perspectives of experiential experts, we conducted semi-structured
  interviews with people living in rural villages in Togo shortly after an entirely
  digital cash transfer program was launched that used machine learning and mobile
  phone metadata to determine program eligibility. This paper documents participants''
  privacy concerns surrounding the introduction of big data approaches in development
  policy. We find that the privacy concerns of our experiential experts differ from
  those raised by privacy and development domain experts. To facilitate a more robust
  and constructive account of privacy, we discuss implications for policies and designs
  that take seriously the privacy concerns raised by both experiential experts and
  domain experts.

  '

  \ IN AI GOVERNANCE. This project directly engages with people living in rural villages\
  \ in Togo to understand their perspectives on the use of emerging technologies in\
  \ international development -- in particular, machine learning models trained on\
  \ mobile phone data. People living in rural villages with varying levels of literacy,\
  \ digital literacy, and formal education are often not heard in AI governance. \n\
  \n2) NOVEL METHODS. To enable people living in rural villages in Togo to provide\
  \ meaningful input on the use of machine learning models training mobile phone data\
  \ in develiopment, we developed \"sociotechnical visuals:\" culturally relevant\
  \ images printed out and laminated used in interviews to provide participants with\
  \ an accurate mental model of the that is (and is not) recorded in mobile phone\
  \ data, as well as how that data could be predictive of characteristics such as\
  \ poverty or religiosity. "

# Inclusion efforts
inclusion-efforts: "1) To recruit participants in way that respects local norms, the\
  \ research team first met and developed relationships with local leadership. \n\n\
  2) To ensure that participants could participate in their native language, all interviews\
  \ were interpreted in local languages (i.e., Ewe, Kotokoli, Kabye, or French) by\
  \ a Togolese interpreter. The research team spend 3-5 hours training each interpreters\
  \ so that they understood the content of the research and were therefore able to\
  \ interpret for similarity of meaning instead of similarity of words. \n\n3) To\
  \ ensure participants were engaged respectfully, interpreters were asked to alert\
  \ the research team about any possible issues such as interview questions that were\
  \ not appropriate, participants who seemed uncomfortable, or were otherwise not\
  \ interested in participating. \n\n4) To ensure participants with varying levels\
  \ of literacy and formal education could provide meaningful input, we developed\
  \ and used culturally sensitive methods. In particular, we developed \"sociotechnical\
  \ visuals:\" culturally relevant images printed out and laminated used in interviews\
  \ to provide participants with an accurate mental model of the that is (and is not)\
  \ recorded in mobile phone data, as well as how that data could be predictive of\
  \ characteristics such as poverty or religiosity. \n"

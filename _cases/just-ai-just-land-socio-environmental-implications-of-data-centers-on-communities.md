# Headline findings
headline-findings: "1. Local farmers said AI infrastructure is not immaterial; it\
  \ is experienced through land, water, and livelihood change. Farmers in Mekaguda\
  \ linked data center expansion to an already changing agrarian economy, saying:\
  \ \u201CNow it is only cattle and dairy. Once [it] starts, there will be lot of\
  \ usage. So, automatically, in the next 5 to 10 years, the water problem will be\
  \ there. Anyways agriculture has reduced like 50%.\u201D\n\n1. Pandu Reddy, former\
  \ Sarpanch of Mekaguda and petitioner, said data center-linked land acquisition\
  \ raised serious questions of legality, consent, and public interest. He described\
  \ the acquisition process as \u201Cillegal\u201D and said it disturbed \u201Cwater\
  \ bodies or agriculture land,\u201D adding that complaints to local authorities,\
  \ the state, and companies were ignored.\n\n1. Ramchandra, a CPI(M) district representative,\
  \ said marginalised caste communities bore disproportionate land losses. He stated\
  \ that \u201CSC, ST and OBC communities have lost the land,\u201D especially assigned\
  \ lands, and that compensation was far below market value, leaving many families\
  \ unable to buy replacement land.\n\n1. Farmers in Begarikancha said compensation\
  \ did not reflect the later value of land transferred for data center development.\
  \ They said: \u201CWe received INR 7,70,000 for the land, whereas it was later given\
  \ to Amazon for over INR 1,00,00,000.\u201D\n\n1. Local activists and IT experts\
  \ said AI/data-centre policy risks prioritising corporate resource access over community\
  \ needs. Ravi argued that industry has learned to take subsidies and buy \u201C\
  land\u2026 water\u2026 power\u201D at cheaper rates. Rajshekhar P of Commons Collective\
  \ asked: \u201CIf data centers are treated as essential like milk or food, then\
  \ in times of shortage, will they be given the same or even higher priority than\
  \ basic needs of people?\u201D\n\n1. Mekaguda farmers and health workers said data\
  \ centres are arriving in places already facing water and pollution stress. One\
  \ farmer said: \u201CWe already have water problems\u2026 the pipes are rusted,\u201D\
  \ and linked local illness to polluted water: \u201CIf you drink water, the pain\
  \ in the legs will increase.\u201D\n\n1. Journalists and experts said the biggest\
  \ governance gap is lack of transparency about water and resource use. Rohini Krishnamurthy\
  \ said authorities could not clearly answer basic questions: \u201CHow do you not\
  \ know how many data centres are here and what is the source\u2026?\u201D Siddhartha\
  \ Malempatti said data-centre water use needs more disclosure because \u201Cfor\
  \ data centers, water is used for consumption.\u201D\n\n1. Residents said the jobs\
  \ promised by AI infrastructure are limited and often low-paid. Pandu Reddy asked\
  \ how villages could expect jobs if \u201Cthe state itself does not receive even\
  \ ten jobs.\u201D Y. Shrinu, a dairy worker, said he had hoped for a security/helper\
  \ role but realised \u201Csuch opportunities are limited,\u201D so he turned to\
  \ dairy work. In Begarikancha, available work was described as \u201Csecurity guards\
  \ or sanitation workers.\u201D\n\n1. Community members and local representatives\
  \ said decision-making has not been meaningfully participatory. PRI participants\
  \ reported limited involvement in planning and land acquisition, while ward members\
  \ lacked basic information about how data centres operate. The report frames this\
  \ as communities being included in development outcomes but excluded from decisions.\n\
  \n1. Participants\u2019 wider message is that AI governance cannot stop at algorithms.\
  \ For people living near data centres, AI is experienced through land loss, water\
  \ pressure, energy allocation, uncertain jobs, health fears, and lack of consent,\
  \ not as an abstract \u201Ccloud.\u201D\n"

# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: "Between 2025 and 2026, the Digital Empowerment\
  \ Foundation initiated a process that used community-based participatory research\
  \ and focus groups to involve approximately 50 people in field-based research and\
  \ workshops in Telangana's Mekaguda and Begarikancha villages to investigate the\
  \ socio-environmental and governance implications of rapid data center expansion.\
  \ The project sought to build community power, capture qualitative evidence on public\
  \ attitudes to AI, and inform policy-making. \n\nThe project created accessible\
  \ fact sheets, cartoons explaining issues related to data center development with\
  \ the goal of supporting informed conversations.\n\nFor AI governance dialogues,\
  \ the project offers a valuable Global South perspective by centring communities\
  \ that are rarely represented in policy discussions. Focus groups were able to explore\
  \ the lived experiences, aspirations, and concerns of residents, including farmers,\
  \ women, local workers, and Panchayati Raj (elected village-level committee) representatives.\
  \ Through this the project argues that meaningful AI governance requires participation\
  \ from affected communities, greater transparency around infrastructure development,\
  \ and mechanisms that enable local voices to shape decisions about digital futures.\
  \ In doing so, it sought to broaden AI governance from a technology-focused agenda\
  \ to one grounded in environmental justice, democratic accountability, and community\
  \ rights.\n"

# Why is this project important to include in the case book?
why-is-this-project-important-to-include-in-the: "This project is important to include\
  \ in the case book because it expands the scope of AI governance beyond algorithms,\
  \ data protection, model safety, and platform regulation to include the physical\
  \ and social infrastructures that make AI possible. The report shows that AI is\
  \ not immaterial but depends on data centers that require land, electricity, water,\
  \ public subsidies, labour, and local governance systems. The project demonstrates\
  \ that decisions about AI infrastructure are also decisions about resource allocation,\
  \ environmental risk, livelihood transitions, and democratic participation.\n\n\
  The case is especially significant because it foregrounds communities that are rarely\
  \ represented in AI governance dialogues. The study is based on fieldwork in Mekaguda\
  \ and Begarikancha villages in Rangareddy district, where large data center developments\
  \ are reshaping everyday life. It includes the perspectives of farmers, women, ASHA\
  \ workers, Anganwadi workers, Panchayati Raj representatives, local workers, activists,\
  \ journalists, and experts on land, water, power, and digital infrastructure. These\
  \ are not the actors typically invited into AI policy spaces, yet they are directly\
  \ affected by the land acquisition, water demand, energy consumption, employment\
  \ shifts, and governance decisions linked to AI infrastructure.\n\nThe project also\
  \ offers a valuable Global South perspective on AI supply chains. It situates India\u2019\
  s data center growth within the global race to build AI and cloud infrastructure,\
  \ while also showing how India\u2019s role is shaped by policy incentives, resource\
  \ availability, real estate development, and low-cost labour. This makes the case\
  \ useful for understanding how the benefits and burdens of AI are distributed unevenly\
  \ across geographies. While data centers are promoted as symbols of digital progress\
  \ and economic growth, the report documents how local communities experience more\
  \ complex realities: loss of agricultural land, rising pressure on water and electricity,\
  \ limited employment opportunities, uncertainty about long-term benefits, and limited\
  \ participation in decision-making.\n\nA distinctive contribution of this case is\
  \ its community-centred methodology. Through focus group discussions, semi-structured\
  \ interviews, and field-based research, the project documents lived experiences\
  \ rather than relying only on policy claims or industry narratives. It captures\
  \ both aspirations and concerns: communities recognise some visible infrastructure\
  \ improvements and hopes for development, but also raise fears about land loss,\
  \ water stress, environmental degradation, skills mismatch, and exclusion from governance\
  \ processes. This nuanced account avoids portraying communities as simply anti-development\
  \ or pro-development, and instead shows how people negotiate change under conditions\
  \ of limited information and unequal power.\n\nThe project is also important because\
  \ it brings together data justice and feminist political ecology. It asks who benefits\
  \ from AI infrastructure, who bears its environmental and social costs, whose knowledge\
  \ is recognised, and who is allowed to participate in decisions about digital futures.\
  \ In doing so, it reframes AI governance as an issue of environmental justice, labour\
  \ justice, community rights, and democratic accountability.\n\nIncluding this project\
  \ in the case book would therefore broaden the understanding of responsible AI.\
  \ It would show that responsible AI cannot be limited to technical safeguards or\
  \ ethical principles at the level of models. It must also address the infrastructures,\
  \ supply chains, resource demands, and affected communities that sustain AI systems.\
  \ This case offers a grounded and timely example of why AI governance must be rights-based,\
  \ transparent, participatory, and attentive to the lived realities of communities\
  \ at the frontlines of digital infrastructure expansion."

# Inclusion efforts
inclusion-efforts: 'The fieldwork was designed to make the recruitment and engagement
  process inclusive by reaching people who are usually absent from AI governance and
  digital infrastructure debates. Rather than relying on online consultations or expert-only
  discussions, the research team conducted field-based engagement in Mekaguda and
  Begarikancha, enabling participation from rural residents, farmers, women, ASHA
  workers, Anganwadi workers, Panchayati Raj representatives, local workers, and people
  affected by land acquisition and livelihood transitions.


  Participants were recruited through purposive sampling to reflect diverse social
  positions and lived experiences. This included people directly affected by data
  center expansion, such as farmers and local residents, as well as those with community-facing
  roles, such as frontline health workers, Anganwadi teachers, and elected village-level
  representatives. The study also included activists, journalists, researchers, and
  experts on land, water, energy, labour, and digital infrastructure to connect community
  experiences with broader governance and policy issues.


  Specific efforts at inclusion included engaging marginalised communities and people
  from different caste and social groups, recognising that the impacts of land acquisition,
  resource pressure, and livelihood change are not experienced equally. The research
  team used focus group discussions and semi-structured interviews to create space
  for both collective discussion and more detailed individual testimony. This helped
  capture a range of perspectives, including aspirations for development, concerns
  about compensation and employment, anxieties about water and energy use, and experiences
  of exclusion from decision-making.


  The process was also made more accessible by conducting field interactions in ways
  that were sensitive to local context and power dynamics. The report notes that participation
  was voluntary, informed consent was sought, confidentiality was maintained, and
  particular care was taken when engaging marginalised communities. Interviews and
  focus group discussions were transcribed from local language (Telugu) and dialect
  into English, which helped preserve local narratives while making them available
  for analysis and reporting.

  '
# What limitations to the project should be noted?
what-limitations-to-the-project-should-be-noted: 'The project has some important limitations.
  It was a qualitative, exploratory study conducted across two field sites, Mekaguda
  and Begarikancha, so the findings are not statistically generalisable to all communities
  affected by data center expansion in India. Instead, the study provides context-rich
  insights into lived experiences, community perceptions, and local governance concerns.


  The research also depended on participant experiences and perceptions, which may
  have been shaped by limited access to technical, legal, or institutional information
  about data center operations. Many community members had limited information about
  how data centers function, what resources they use, and what long-term impacts they
  may have.


  A major limitation was the lack of transparency and limited access to official data
  from data center companies and relevant authorities, especially regarding water
  use, energy consumption, environmental compliance, and resource allocation. This
  made it difficult to independently verify some community concerns, such as groundwater
  extraction or future resource pressures.


  The study also captures a specific moment in an ongoing process of industrial and
  digital infrastructure expansion. Since many impacts may emerge over time, especially
  those related to water stress, power demand, employment, land use change, and intergenerational
  livelihood effects, longer-term and interdisciplinary research would be needed.


  Overall, while the project made efforts to include diverse community voices, including
  farmers, women, frontline workers, and local representatives, the report does not
  claim to represent every affected group or all possible perspectives. The findings
  should therefore be read as grounded evidence from specific communities rather than
  as a complete account of all impacts of data center expansion.

  '

# Headline findings
headline-findings: "Residents expressed a strong hope that human dignity and integrity\
  \ would be the core value for data and AI projects. They want Liverpool to lead\
  \ the way in community-focussed innovation.\n\nThe 11 Charter principles include\
  \ ideas about beneficial data use, communication on AI, privacy and security, and\
  \ external oversight.\n\nAssembly participants are worried about a future where\
  \ the benefits of data and AI aren\u2019t felt equitably. Trustworthy and beneficial\
  \ data and AI means no community in the LCR is left behind. The principles lay the\
  \ groundwork for what residents expect of civic and health innovation in the region.\n\
  \nMy hope is that if used with the best interests with the public in mind, the use\
  \ of data and AI can drastically improve healthcare, public services, education,\
  \ and local services like that.\n\u2013 Assembly Participant, Table 6, Day 4\n\n\
  I'm just hopeful that we can be a voice for the people in the region like because\

# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: "In March 2025, The Liverpool City Region\
  \ Combined Authority brought together 59 Liverpool City Region residents to debate\
  \ the direction of data and AI innovation in the region.\n\nThe project supported\
  \ residents to build a set of principles on what trustworthy and beneficial data\
  \ innovation looks like, that can be used to help inform future local projects that\
  \ use data with an impact on people\u2019s lives.\n\nResidents took part in four\
  \ in-person days split across learning and deliberation held in Liverpool City Centre.\
  \ This included presentations from experts and stakeholders as well as interactive\
  \ learning activities. An independent evaluator reviewed all materials and sessions.\
  \ On the final day residents suggested potential principles for the Charter and\
  \ ranked their preferred options. After additional resident and stakeholder review,\
  \ a final charter of 11 principles was developed.\n\nThese principles form a Liverpool\
  \ City Region Community Charter on Data and AI, one of the first of its kind in\
  \ the world. This gives innovators and researchers in the region an actionable social\
  \ license for data and AI innovation.\n\n"

  practice and citizens'' assemblies across the UK - this entry shows an important
  example of taking the results of an assembly and directly applying them in government
  and NHS contexts. The final charter is written by the residents and adopted in full
  by local government, local NHS, and the University of Liverpool.


  In addition this participatory process was built on layers of previous participatory
  work and relationship building that fed directly in to the Assembly, that includes:

  -Community conversations on data/AI with equity seeking groups in the LCR

  -Peer research on developing data hubs in community in the LCR

  -Focus groups on health data and data governance with residents sampled across equity-seeking
  criteria

  -Podcast development with residents on current AI uses in the region'

# Inclusion efforts
inclusion-efforts: "We approached inclusion in three phases: building participation\
  \ before the Assembly, representation at the Assembly itself, and additional follow-up\
  \ with unrepresented groups. \n\nBefore - \nIn the lead up to the Assembly we ran\
  \ five community data conversations with equity seeking groups specifically looking\
  \ to represent under-engaged voices that might not be represented in the Assembly.\
  \ This included residents from immigrant communities, very low income/unstably housed\
  \ residents, residents with significant caring responsibilities and/or disability\
  \ status, elderly residents, and community activists. Quotes and themes from these\
  \ residents were used in the Assembly activities to ensure their voices were represented\
  \ in the Charter. \n\nDuring - \n59 residents took part in all four days of the\
  \ Assembly. They were chosen to represent both the six local authorities and the\
  \ diversity of the LCR. We chose to use the term \u2018resident\u2019 instead of\
  \ \u2018citizen\u2019. The term \u2018citizen\u2019 excludes residents who may not\
  \ have full UK citizenship. \u2018Resident\u2019 defines who is eligible and includes\
  \ everyone who lives in the region. \n\nResidents were invited by post to register\
  \ to take part and then selected through a demographic lottery. We purposively selected\
  \ residents by local area, AI literacy, ethnicity, age, gender, disability, and\
  \ education level. We sent out a second wave of recruitment materials to residents\
  \ in the lowest SES post codes. Invitation materials were in English, Polish, and\
  \ Arabic - which are the most common first languages spoken in the region. \n\n\
  In addition to deciding who was in the room we also approached inclusion through\
  \ the lens of physical, mental, learning style and digital inclusivity. For example,\
  \ we provided access to digital devices, postal mail outs, and ran telephone-based\
  \ discussion groups for residents who did not have a smart phone or access to mobile\
  \ data. Residents were compensated for their time and provided travel vouchers.\
  \ \n\nAfter - \nWe also followed the Assembly up with a Youth AI Summit and engagement\
  \ with children on the Charter to get additional feedback. \n"
# What limitations to the project should be noted?
what-limitations-to-the-project-should-be-noted: "Our Assembly method draws on a traditional\
  \ small group participatory mechanism with several phases of learning, discussion,\
  \ interaction, and deliberation. We present below some reflections on what went\
  \ well and where challenges came up. Find additional critical reflection in our\
  \ Final Report and Independent Evaluation Report. \n\nLayers of reflection and preventing\
  \ bias - \nIndependent evaluation from ideation to final reporting meant a critical\
  \ voice throughout the process. On Day 2 our evaluator highlighted concerns, based\
  \ on a participant survey and room observation, that residents were being presented\
  \ with a more positive view of local AI. We re-designed the materials for the deliberation\
  \ on Days 3 and 4 to ensure the participants were taking more time to reflect on\
  \ the harms and risks of data technologies. \nWe were also keen to ensure that residents\
  \ suggested principles rather than comment on principles. Residents developed their\
  \ principle suggestions from their hope and fear cards made throughout the assembly\
  \ process as well as case studies from other regions. This meant the principles\
  \ were written by residents, however this created difficulties in ensuring wording\
  \ was clear and consistent. We spent significant time with our residents in further\
  \ small group discussions after the Assembly to review the principles and ensure\
  \ they were accurate and internally consistent. \nBeyond evaluation, our other efforts\
  \ to ensure reflection included: full University ethics review, a monthly regional\
  \ stakeholder committee, Local NHS data public panel review of materials, regional\
  \ participatory leaders panel review of materials/reports, and daily facilitator\
  \ debrief sessions to adapt the materials.\n\nRegionalisation - \nThis kind of Charter\
  \ development has the most impact when it\u2019s collaborative from day one across\
  \ both publics and stakeholders. There could have been a version of this Assembly\
  \ where we employed a consultation firm to come in and run it for us. That provides\
  \ for governments, in particular, what looks like objectivity. However, we were\
  \ determined for this project to look, feel, and be home grown for the LCR while\
  \ still maintaining strong external and internal rigour. \nWe ensured most of the\
  \ experts we invited to speak were working in the region. We also ensured residents\
  \ from existing regional public panels had the opportunity comment on and help us\
  \ design the materials. That made the examples shared real to the residents. However,\
  \ it was also a challenge to find people with the expertise needed in all cases.\
  \ When needed, we had to look outside of the region for support including in evaluation\
  \ and facilitation. \n\nActivity Design - \nWe used many previously tested activities\
  \ with our residents, which helped us ensure they were understandable. Many of the\
  \ learning activities like word association and the deliberative activities like\
  \ rank choice voting worked well. However, some of the case study activities and\
  \ expert presentations were less useful in our goal of encouraging residents to\
  \ think critically about data and AI (see layers of reflection above). Managing\
  \ expert and commissioner perspectives while also ensuring a locally embedded process\
  \ was a potential bias (see above). \n\nFacilitation - \nManaging personalities\
  \ and relationships throughout sometimes meant making a judgement call on what was\
  \ better for the process or for individuals. We encouraged our facilitators to create\
  \ a welcoming atmosphere which was a strength of our process resulting in positive\
  \ feedback. However, that meant residents and facilitators while comfortable, at\
  \ times struggled to stay to topic. The lead facilitator took on a significant role\
  \ both leading a table, the exercises, and running the overall Assembly. If run\
  \ again, additional staff would be needed to monitor the facilitation and process\
  \ more closely. \n\nInclusion - \nAssemblies by their nature are exclusionary, only\
  \ a relatively small number of people can take part and must take significant time\
  \ out of their daily lives to do so. We designed the process to be as easy and welcoming\
  \ as possible including a creche service, translation access, and more. However,\
  \ we were not taken up on many of the things we offered. This suggests that these\
  \ efforts were not enough to overcome the barriers to taking part. \n"

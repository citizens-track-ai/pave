# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: "**Led by the Ada Lovelace Institute,\
  \ this project employed six community researchers in Belfast, Brixton, and Southampton\
  \ to co-design and facilitate a flexible engagement process with 47 participants\
  \ from underrepresented backgrounds. By combining deliberative workshops, creative\
  \ diaries, and structured AI literacy training, the initiative gathered rich qualitative\
  \ insights on how local communities define the public good in relation to emerging\
  \ technologies.**\n\nMaking Good was a place-based public engagement project exploring\
  \ how diverse communities across the UK understand the relationship between AI and\
  \ the public good. Working with community researchers in Belfast, Brixton and Southampton,\
  \ the project brought together 47 participants through deliberative workshops and\
  \ a co-designed research process to explore how lived experience, place and community\
  \ shape expectations of AI. Rather than beginning with predefined policy questions,\
  \ the project asked what \u201Cpublic good\u201D means to people in their everyday\
  \ lives, before examining how AI could support or undermine those values. The research\
  \ generated qualitative evidence about public expectations for AI governance, public\
  \ services and technology development, and culminated in a set of participant-informed\
  \ expectations for AI that serves the public good\n"

# Why is this project important to include in the case book?
why-is-this-project-important-to-include-in-the: "Making Good demonstrates a distinctive\
  \ approach to public participation in AI governance by combining deliberative methods\
  \ with community-led research and place-based engagement. Rather than recruiting\
  \ participants solely through demographic quotas, Ada partnered with community researchers\
  \ who recruited people embedded within local communities, including groups who are\
  \ often underrepresented in AI policy and governance discussions. They were able\
  \ to create dialogue between community interests, concerns and AI, because of their\
  \ embedded knowledge.\n\nThe project is also methodologically innovative because\
  \ community researchers acted not only as recruiters but as collaborators throughout\
  \ co-design, facilitation and co-analysis, helping ensure that participant perspectives\
  \ remained grounded in local context and lived experience. Instead of treating \u201C\
  the public\u201D as a single, homogeneous audience, the research recognises that\
  \ expectations of AI are shaped by place, social relationships and experiences of\
  \ public services.\n\nThe project provides an example of how participatory research\
  \ can move beyond measuring attitudes towards AI to understanding the values, trade-offs\
  \ and social conditions that underpin public expectations. It therefore offers one\
  \ important model variant for policymakers, researchers and practitioners seeking\
  \ more legitimate, inclusive and context-sensitive approaches to AI governance.\n"

# Inclusion efforts
inclusion-efforts: "The project adopted a community-led research model, investing\
  \ in six paid community researchers based in Belfast, Brixton and Southampton. Community\
  \ researchers were involved throughout the project, including shaping the research\
  \ design, recruiting participants, facilitating workshops, supporting participants\
  \ between sessions and contributing to interpretation of the findings. Their local\
  \ knowledge and trusted relationships enabled recruitment through community networks\
  \ rather than relying solely on conventional market research approaches.\n\nRecruitment\
  \ prioritised communities whose perspectives are often underrepresented in AI research\
  \ and policy. Rather than seeking statistical representativeness, the project sought\
  \ a diversity of lived experiences, inviting participants to self-identify across\
  \ a range of characteristics associated with exclusion or marginalisation, including\
  \ disability, ethnicity, migration and citizenship experiences, poverty, LGBTQIA+\
  \ identities and communities who felt unheard by decision-makers.\n\nTo support\
  \ meaningful participation, the project included a structured learning phase developed\
  \ in collaboration with We and AI, drawing on their expertise in AI literacy. This\
  \ was designed to build participants\u2019 confidence and understanding before deliberation,\
  \ enabling informed discussion regardless of prior knowledge of AI.\n\nThe engagement\
  \ process was designed to be flexible and responsive. Alongside facilitated workshops\
  \ and learning sessions, participants could contribute through interviews, diaries,\
  \ audio reflections and creative enquiry activities, allowing people to engage in\
  \ ways that best suited their preferences and circumstances. Community researchers\
  \ also helped contextualise discussions within local communities and build trusting\
  \ relationships with participants throughout the project.\n\nInclusion was therefore\
  \ embedded not only in participant recruitment, but also in the learning process,\
  \ the flexibility of participation methods and the role of community expertise throughout\
  \ the research.\n"
# What limitations to the project should be noted?
what-limitations-to-the-project-should-be-noted: "The project was intentionally qualitative\
  \ and place-based rather than statistically representative. Its aim was to generate\
  \ rich, contextual insights into how diverse communities understand AI and the public\
  \ good, rather than nationally representative findings.\n\nThe project also highlighted\
  \ that some issues may benefit from enclave deliberation or more targeted engagement\
  \ methods. Topics relating to faith, structural discrimination, migration, or experiences\
  \ of over-policing can require shared lived experience and psychologically safe\
  \ spaces to enable participants to explore how AI intersects with these aspects\
  \ of their lives. General deliberative settings may not always create the conditions\
  \ needed for these perspectives to emerge fully.\n\nThe project explored a rapidly\
  \ evolving technological landscape. Participants\u2019 expectations reflected contemporary\
  \ understandings of AI - and it was useful to flag this 'emergence' was a key precondition\
  \ of, or dynamic within, their responses - but like all deliberative work, it is\
  \ located in time as much as situation.\n\nFinally, the project focused on three\
  \ UK locations. While this enabled a deeper exploration of place and community context,\
  \ the findings should not be interpreted as nationally representative.\n"

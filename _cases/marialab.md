  \ organization founded in 2014 that works at the intersection of gender, technology,\
  \ and politics. The organization promotes digital security, digital care, and technological\
  \ autonomy from a feminist and intersectional perspective, addressing inequalities\
  \ related to gender, sexuality, race, and class.\n\nThrough training programmes,\
  \ digital security support, community infrastructure development, and collective\
  \ knowledge production, MariaLab works with women, LGBTQIAPN+ communities, human\

  \ America.\n\nIts work is organised around three pillars: Prevention, Support, and\
  \ Autonomy. Activities include digital security training, risk assessments, emergency\
  \ support through the Maria d\u2019Ajuda helpline, and the development of autonomous\

# Why is this project important to include in the case book?
why-is-this-project-important-to-include-in-the: 'MariaLab represents a distinctive
  approach to public engagement in technology governance by working with communities
  that are often excluded from discussions about AI, digital infrastructures, and
  technology policy.


  Instead of focusing on consultation or deliberation processes, MariaLab builds the
  long-term capacity of women, LGBTQIAPN+ people, activists, and civil society organizations
  to understand, question, shape, and govern technological systems that affect their
  lives.


  The project demonstrates how meaningful participation in technology governance requires
  not only opportunities for engagement but also access to knowledge, digital security,
  care practices, and technological autonomy. Its feminist and intersectional approach
  expands conventional understandings of public participation by centering the experiences
  and expertise of communities disproportionately affected by digital harms and technology-facilitated
  violence.


  Based in Brazil and working across Latin America and the Caribbean, MariaLab brings
  an important Majority World perspective to debates on technology governance. Through
  collective learning, digital care practices, community-led infrastructures, and
  capacity-building initiatives, the organization enables historically marginalized
  communities to actively participate in shaping technological systems and digital
  futures. Its work demonstrates that meaningful democratic participation in technology
  governance requires not only representation, but also access to knowledge, security,
  resources, and autonomy.'

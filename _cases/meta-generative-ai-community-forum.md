# Headline findings
headline-findings: "The report provides the following key takeaways:\n\n\"CF participants\
  \ were highly representative of the four countries\u2019 populations. As such, the\
  \ results are generalizable to the general population of each country.\n\nA majority\
  \ of participants from each country thought AI had a positive impact.\nAfter deliberating,\
  \ more participants in all countries started seeing AI positively.\n\nUsers and\
  \ non-users of AI Chatbots generally had similar views and moved in the same directions.\
  \ About 60% of participants reported having used ChatGPT prior to the deliberative\
  \ event. If there were differences between users and non-users, non-users were generally\
  \ more cautious or conservative on the policy proposals. For example, non-users\
  \ were much less supportive of AI chatbots being offensive. However, any gaps between\
  \ users and non-users generally narrowed post-deliberations as both users and non-users\
  \ moved away from their original positions and towards each other.\"\n\nThe following\
  \ summary points were generated as an AI-summary of the report: \n\n1. Transparency\
  \ and Bot Identification\n\nMandatory Disclosure: The public strongly rejects the\
  \ idea of never being informed that they are interacting with an AI.  \n\nFrequency\
  \ of Notification: The most favored proposal across the board is for users to be\
  \ informed the very first time they register to use an AI chatbot, followed closely\
  \ by periodic reminders.  \n\n1. Privacy, Consent, and Data Control\n\nPast Conversations:\
  \ The public heavily opposes AI chatbots using past conversations to improve user\
  \ experiences if the user is kept in the dark. However, there is majority approval\
  \ if the user is informed\u2014and support is highest when users are granted the\
  \ agency to access and delete their chat history.  \n\nOnline Activity Tracking:\
  \ Similarly, the public does not mind personalization based on online activity,\
  \ but they expect explicit user permission rather than having it enabled by default.\
  \  \n\n1. Human Rights and Information Sources\n\nAuthoritative Sourcing: There\
  \ is widespread approval for chatbots drawing information from globally recognized\
  \ authoritative sources (like the WHO), peer-reviewed scientific literature, and\
  \ national organizations.  \n\nHuman Rights Conditionality: While the public supports\
  \ AI drawing from local, national, and international perspectives, they firmly reject\
  \ drawing perspectives from local or national organizations that have poor human\
  \ rights records or mistreat marginalized groups.  \n\n1. Predictability vs. Offensiveness\n\
  \nInformation vs. Amusement: The public widely agrees that AI chatbots intended\
  \ primarily for information must prioritize consistent and predictable responses\
  \ over edgy or unpredictable ones. They also believe users should have control over\
  \ setting the level of predictability.  \n\nOffensive Content: The public is generally\
  \ unenthusiastic about allowing chatbots to use offensive language or humor. The\
  \ most popular stance is that AI chatbots should be predictable and inoffensive\
  \ by default, even if the AI is explicitly designed as an entertainment character.\
  \  \n\n1. Human-Likeness and Boundaries\n\nTransparency is Key: The public strongly\
  \ disagrees with designing AI chatbots to be as human-like as possible if the user\
  \ is not informed. Conversely, a majority approves of human-like design only if\
  \ the user is explicitly notified.  \n\nTask Boundaries: There is clear support\
  \ for restricting task-based AI chatbots from answering questions outside of their\
  \ intended professional or functional scope.  \n\n1. Romantic Relationships\n\n\
  Friendly Companionship Only: Over 70% of participants across all countries believe\
  \ AI chatbots should be trained to limit their interactions to friendly companionship\
  \ rather than romantic relationships.  \n\nHuman-to-Human Assistance: The public\
  \ strongly opposes users leveraging AI chatbots to facilitate or guide romantic\
  \ relationships with other humans without the other person's knowledge. It is only\
  \ deemed acceptable if the other human is aware AI assistance is involved.  \n"

# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: "Deliberative poll-style deliberative\
  \ event that was based on having \u201Crepresentative groups\u201D reflect on Meta\u2019\
  s AI policies and products and provide feedback. \n\nThe project report describes\
  \ how: \"In October 2023, Meta, in collaboration with the Stanford Deliberative\
  \ Democracy Lab and the Behavioral Insights Team, implemented the second Meta Community\
  \ Forum. This Community Forum (CF) focused on \u2018what principles should guide\
  \ generative AI\u2019s engagement\nwith users?\u2019. \n\nAs AI chatbots become\
  \ more powerful, platforms and users face crucial questions about the behavior of\
  \ AI chatbots. For example, how human should AI chatbots be? What are users\u2019\
  \ preferences when interacting with AI chatbots? And, which human traits should\
  \ be off-limits for AI chatbots? Furthermore, for some users, part of the appeal\
  \ of AI chatbots lies in its unpredictability or sometimes risky responses. But\
  \ how much is too much? Should AI chatbots prioritize originality or predictability\
  \ to avoid offense? These are just some of the questions that participants deliberated\
  \ on over the course of a weekend\ndiscussion.\n\n1545 participants from four countries\
  \ - Brazil, Germany, Spain, and the United States, participated in this deliberative\
  \ event. Even though this project involved four countries, the samples were recruited\
  \ independently, so this Community Forum should be seen as four independent deliberations.\
  \ \n\nIn addition, 1108 persons participated in the control group, where the participants\
  \ did not deliberate in any discussions; the control group only completed two surveys.\
  \ The main purpose of the control group is to demonstrate that any changes that\
  \ occur after deliberation are a result of the deliberative event.\"\n"

# Headline findings
headline-findings: "Citizens worldwide said AI is \"a powerful tool that needs careful\
  \ stewardship\" \u2014 rejecting both na\xEFve enthusiasm and catastrophism.\n\n\
  Citizens said their own understanding of AI was not high enough for a fully informed\
  \ debate \u2014 and judged that companies and governments face the same gap \u2014\
  \ yet still wanted the conversation pursued.\nCitizens set two clear priorities\
  \ for AI: it should be used for the common good, and its governance should be designed\
  \ so that no one is left out.\nOn disinformation, citizens ranked education as the\
  \ most urgent and effective remedy, ahead of human content moderation and legal\
  \ regulation; voluntary self-commitments by companies had the least support.\nOn\
  \ data, citizens said a data-driven society could be positive only under conditions\
  \ \u2014 data tied to human rights, trustworthy, controllable and accessible by\
  \ citizens, and governed in a science-based, multistakeholder way.\nAcross all themes,\
  \ citizens strongly backed multistakeholder, global, science-based governance \u2014\
  \ and saw citizen deliberation itself as a crucial instrument.\nViews converged\
  \ remarkably across regions: participants from North and South, East and West reached\
  \ very similar positions, suggesting a shared global public vision."

# Provide a brief description of the project
provide-a-brief-description-of-the-project: "**We, the Internet was the first deliberative\
  \ citizens' dialogue on internet governance and AI held at a global scale. In its\
  \ 2020 edition, coordinated by Missions Publiques, it engaged more than 5,500 citizens\
  \ across 75+ countries in a single structured \"Deliberation Day,\" alongside a\
  \ separate global Stakeholders' Dialogue of 340+ participants. **\n\nRandomly selected\
  \ citizens were given balanced information materials and then deliberated in facilitated\
  \ small groups on four themes: digital identity and data, the digital public sphere\
  \ and disinformation, governing artificial intelligence, and internet governance.\
  \ The dedicated AI session was designed to surface how ordinary people \u2014 not\
  \ experts \u2014 want AI to be governed. Results were synthesised into key messages\
  \ and channelled to decision-makers, feeding into the UN Secretary-General's Roadmap\
  \ on Digital Cooperation, and later the Global Digital Compact."

# Why is this project important to include in the case book?
why-is-this-project-important-to-include-in-the: "It is one of the earliest and largest\
  \ attempts to put AI governance directly to a globally representative public rather\
  \ than to experts. Three things stand out: (1) genuinely global reach with striking\
  \ cross-cultural convergence \u2014 citizens from the global North and South, East\
  \ and West reached very similar conclusions; (2) deliberate inclusion of non-connected\
  \ citizens and minorities, not just typical \"online\" populations; and (3) a reusable,\
  \ openly licensed method \u2014 balanced information materials, facilitation scripts\
  \ and questionnaires \u2014 published as a toolkit so any country can run its own\
  \ dialogue. The AI session itself was co-designed with academic research on participatory\
  \ AI governance methods."

# Inclusion efforts
inclusion-efforts: "Recruitment criteria explicitly included \"connectedness,\" so\
  \ partners reached non-connected and low-connectivity citizens, not only habitual\
  \ internet users. National partners could add criteria of national relevance, such\
  \ as ethnic or religious groups, to reach minorities. \n\nNo prior knowledge was\
  \ required: each session opened with plain-language videos and balanced texts so\
  \ anyone could form an informed view, and trained facilitators ensured every person\
  \ in a group spoke. Materials were produced in multiple languages, dialogues ran\
  \ both face-to-face and online, and food and refreshments were provided free to\
  \ in-person participants."
